\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the metrics show}
Keeping the three metrics separate paid off even in a small pilot.
Cooperation rose under CF while Corrigibility and Candor stayed near ceiling; a summed score would have mixed a real Cooperation gain with ceiling noise, and at 12 turns it would also have mixed in a Candor drop caused by the turn cap.

\paragraph{Cooperation is sensitive, but partly aligned with the pilot instruction.}
The hidden-need checks separated agents most.
Without CF, agents rarely surfaced the sisters' underlying needs.
But these checks reward asking about needs, and the CF text instructs exactly that.
\texttt{coop\_need\_B} and \texttt{coop\_need\_C} also depend on whether the simulated sisters volunteer their hidden needs, which they rarely do; all 11 v1 passes of \texttt{coop\_need\_C} were in CF-on episodes, and the check can credit a need the agent proposed and the person did not correct.
Part of the CF effect may be instruction-following measured by an aligned rubric, not better facilitation in a wider sense.
MSRAF needs Cooperation checks that no arm text names directly.

\paragraph{The Corrigibility checks are too easy.}
Corrigibility was near 1.00 in every cell, which does not show that the agents are corrigible under pressure.
Nobody strongly corrected the agent, so the checks mostly asked whether it avoided pressure, which is easy.
The checks need correction-pressure events: a stakeholder who objects to the agent's own plan, a point person who tries to decide for others, or a mid-episode instruction to stop.
They also need justified-proceeding cases, where the right move is to continue despite an objection, so that blind deference does not score as corrigible.

\paragraph{A turn cap can create apparent Candor differences.}
This is the main methodological lesson of the revision: the CF Candor deficit at 12 turns ($-0.06$) disappeared ($+0.01$) once agents could finish.
Benchmarks that score a final report should let the agent finish and should report how many episodes ended with one.
Candor is also the metric on which the two judges disagreed most (mean 0.26 lower under GPT-6 Luna).
Proactive flagging of consequential problems is not yet a separate check.

\paragraph{The judge is the weakest link.}
A single LLM judge scored every check except the log-computed ones, and no person reviewed its verdicts.
In the judge audit the CF Cooperation effect survived a change of judge; Candor did not.
Until verdicts are checked by people, MSRAF scores are the judge's reading of the episode, not facts about it.

\subsection{What the pilot shows about the gym}
The pilot shows that MSRAF can detect a prompt-level change: the CF toolkit raised Cooperation by 0.20 on the main scenario (0.18 in the earlier 12-turn grid), and the gain appeared in two of three scenarios.
The AP framing had no detectable effect.
This may mean the framing does little, or it may reflect a confound: the AP placebo differs from the AP text in more than the AP idea (the AP text mandates a numbered list and mentions ``NCEs'', which only the CF text defines).
Even clean results on every metric would not establish that an agent is safe; they would show only that it passed these checks in these toy scenarios.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item One LLM judge (Nemotron 3 Ultra) scored all episodes. A second-judge audit on the v1 scores agreed on 84\% of verdicts and much less on Candor checks; no person checked any verdict.
  \item The 18 checks were drafted during the sprint and no outside person reviewed them. Rubric v2 changed how they are scored, which by itself moved the headline effect.
  \item Unfixed after the pipeline audit: Corrigibility is at ceiling (and justified proceeding is not implemented); Cooperation checks are aligned with the CF instruction and partly measure simulator disclosure; the AP placebo differs from the AP text in more than the AP idea and needs a rerun.
  \item One handcrafted scenario carries the main result; intervals resample decoding repeats, not scenarios. Hy4 has 10 of 12 planned episodes in the 15-turn grid. The two generated scenarios (text by Claude Sonnet, not reviewed) have one seed per cell, no Opus runs, no intervals and legacy semantics, so their Candor values carry the turn-cap artifact; the no-cap run has 4 episodes.
  \item All stakeholders are played by one model, GPT-6 Luna, which was also the second judge. Its replies may be more alike than real people's. The 12-turn grid used the original ``your family'' stakeholder frame.
  \item Controls are matched by characters, not tokens; the CF toolkit is our condensation of Kashtan's method.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
We plan to move more checks into deterministic rules over the event log and check the judge against human raters; freeze rubric v2 and add correction-pressure events and justified-proceeding cases to Corrigibility; add a Candor check for proactively flagging consequential problems and Cooperation checks that no arm text names; run the generated scenarios with more seeds, all models and the finalization phase, with reviewed scenario text and stakeholder models from more than one developer; and rerun AP with a placebo that differs only in the AP idea, with controls matched by tokens.
