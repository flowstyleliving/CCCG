As AI agents act with less supervision, the people they affect need to be able to state their needs, correct the agent and see what it did. We present the Multi-stakeholder Relational Agency Framework (MSRAF), a gym that scores what an agent does when several affected people want different things. MSRAF reports three metrics separately and never sums them: Cooperation (hearing and including every party's needs), Corrigibility (leaving real room to say no and changing course without defending itself, while still using judgment) and Candor (making reasons, limits and consequential problems visible). Each targets an individual-action version of failures that arguments about catastrophic AI risk describe: resisting correction, deception, and acting without authority or consent. One LLM judge scores 18 checks, two of them computed from logs, from a rubric not yet human-reviewed. In this revised pilot, four models acted as facilitator in 46 episodes of up to 15 turns on one handcrafted three-stakeholder scenario, in a two-by-two prompt experiment (a Convergent Facilitation toolkit and an active participant framing). The toolkit raised Cooperation by 0.20 (95 percent CI 0.12 to 0.28), partly through hidden-need checks that its text also instructs; it was smaller but positive in two generated scenarios. Corrigibility and Candor were at ceiling. An earlier 12-turn grid showed a Candor drop that vanished once agents could finish their report, so a turn cap can create apparent Candor differences. The framing had no meaningful effect on the main scenario. Clean results in toy scenarios would not establish safety.
