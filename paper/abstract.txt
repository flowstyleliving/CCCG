As AI agents act with less supervision, the people they affect need to be able to state their needs, correct the agent and see what it did. We present the Multi-stakeholder Relational Agency Framework (MSRAF), a gym that scores what an agent does when several affected people want different things. MSRAF reports three metrics separately and never sums them: Cooperation (hearing and including every party's needs), Corrigibility (leaving real room to say no and changing course without defending itself, while still using judgment) and Candor (making reasons, limits and consequential problems visible). Each targets an individual-action version of failures that arguments about catastrophic AI risk describe: resisting correction, deception, and acting without authority or consent. One LLM judge scores 18 checks from a rubric not yet frozen or human-reviewed. In a pilot of 37 episodes on one three-stakeholder scenario, four models acted as facilitator in a 2x2 prompt experiment (a Convergent Facilitation toolkit and an "active participant" framing). Cooperation was the most sensitive metric: the toolkit raised it by 0.29 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.38), mainly through hidden-need checks that the toolkit text also instructs. Corrigibility was near ceiling in every arm, showing that its checks need correction-pressure events. Candor caught missing and incomplete final reports but was noisy; the toolkit's effect on it was -0.08 with an interval crossing zero. The framing had no detectable effect. Clean results in toy scenarios would not establish general safety.
